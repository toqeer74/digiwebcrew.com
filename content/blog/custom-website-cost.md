Ask five agencies what a website costs and you'll get five refusals to answer until you book a call. That's a sales tactic, not a pricing reality. The ranges are knowable, and you should walk into any conversation already knowing them.

Here's what we charge, what the wider market charges, and — more usefully — the seven things that actually move the number.

## The honest ranges

For a custom-built marketing site in the US/UK market in 2026:

| Tier | Range | What you actually get |
|---|---|---|
| Template customisation | $800 – $2,500 | A premium theme, your content, minor CSS changes. Not custom. |
| Custom small business site | $3,500 – $9,000 | Bespoke design, 5–15 pages, CMS, real performance work |
| Custom site + funnel/SEO foundation | $9,000 – $25,000 | The above plus conversion architecture, technical SEO, integrations |
| Web application | $25,000 – $150,000+ | Authentication, database, business logic, ongoing state |

Our own starting point is **$3,500 for custom website development**, $2,000 for a focused funnel or landing page, and $2,500 for chatbot and automation work. Those are starting prices, not packages — which matters, and I'll explain why below.

If someone quotes you $500 for a "custom" website, they are selling you a template with your logo dropped in. That's a legitimate product at that price. It just isn't the thing being described.

## The seven factors that actually move the price

### 1. Page count — but less than you'd think

Everyone asks about page count first. It matters least. Pages 6 through 20 are mostly the same layouts with different content. The jump from 5 to 15 pages might add 15% to a build. The jump from a marketing site to an application adds 400%.

### 2. How many unique layouts exist

This is the real version of the page-count question. Ten pages sharing three templates is a small job. Ten pages with ten distinct designs is a big one. When you get a quote, ask how many unique layouts it covers.

### 3. Whether content is ready

The single most common cause of blown timelines. If copy and images arrive mid-build, everything stalls, and stalled projects cost more because the team context-switches away and back. Sites where content is ready at kickoff land roughly 30% faster in our experience.

If your content isn't ready, that's fine — but say so upfront so it's scoped, rather than discovered in week three.

### 4. Integrations

Each external system is a small project. A contact form is trivial. A booking system that syncs to a calendar, fires a CRM record, sends a confirmation email, and handles rescheduling is not. Common integrations and their rough weight:

- Contact form to email: negligible
- Newsletter signup: negligible
- Calendar booking (Calendly-style embed): small
- CRM sync with field mapping: moderate
- Payment processing: moderate to significant
- Custom API to an existing internal system: significant, and highly variable

### 5. Performance requirements

A site that merely works and a site that scores well on [Core Web Vitals](/blog/core-web-vitals-guide) are different engagements. Real performance work — image strategy, font loading, bundle discipline, caching — takes deliberate effort. It's worth paying for, because it's both a ranking factor and a conversion factor. But it isn't free, and shops that quote very low are not doing it.

### 6. Who is actually building it

A $3,000 quote and a $12,000 quote for the same brief usually differ in who touches the code. Offshore template assembly, a mid-level generalist, and a senior engineer produce very different maintenance costs 18 months later. The cheap build often costs more in year two, when nobody can safely change anything.

### 7. Ownership and lock-in

Ask directly: do I own the code, and can another developer take it over? Some low quotes are subsidised by a proprietary platform you can never leave. That's a rental, not a purchase. It may still be the right call for you — just price it as a subscription, not a build.

## Why we use starting prices instead of fixed packages

Fixed packages are comfortable to buy and usually wrong. A packaged "10-page website" charges the law firm with three service areas the same as the clinic with twelve locations and a booking system. One of them is overpaying and the other is about to get a change order.

Starting prices set a floor and then scope the work honestly against what the business actually needs. It's less tidy on a pricing page. It produces fewer unpleasant surprises in week six.

## How to read a quote

Four questions that expose most of the difference between two quotes:

1. **What's explicitly out of scope?** A good quote says what it doesn't include. A vague quote is a change order waiting to happen.
2. **How many rounds of revision are included, and what counts as a round?** "Unlimited revisions" is either a lie or priced defensively.
3. **What happens after launch?** Is there a warranty period? What does a bug cost to fix in month two?
4. **Who owns the code and where does it live?** If the answer is complicated, that's the answer.

## What a website is worth, not what it costs

Cost is only half the decision. A site that converts 2% instead of 1% doubles the return on every marketing dollar you'll ever spend driving traffic to it. For a business where one client is worth $5,000, a single additional conversion per month pays for a $9,000 build in under two months.

That maths only works if the build is genuinely aimed at conversion rather than aesthetics — which is a scoping question, not a budget question. We wrote about the specific version of this problem for [professional services firms](/blog/law-firm-website-conversion).

## Where to start

If you have a rough budget and an unclear scope, the most useful next step is usually a scoping conversation rather than a quote request — you'll get a more accurate number, and you'll find out quickly whether the fit is right.
