Almost every chatbot on a business website makes that website worse. It slides up uninvited, interrupts reading, offers a menu of options that don't match the question, and hands over to a form anyway.

Then there's the small minority that book meetings while the team sleeps. The difference isn't the model. GPT-class models have been good enough for this for two years. The difference is design.

## Why most of them fail

**They interrupt instead of waiting.** A popup at three seconds is an interruption. The same widget, sitting quietly until a visitor has read most of a service page, is an offer. We've seen the timing change alone move engagement by a factor of three.

**They know nothing about the business.** A generic assistant that can't answer "do you work with clinics in California" is worse than no assistant, because it burns the visitor's one unit of patience.

**They optimise for containment, not conversion.** Support chatbots are measured on deflection — resolving without a human. Sales assistants measured the same way will happily talk a hot lead out of booking a call. These are opposite goals, and using a support tool for sales quietly costs you money.

**They collect email and stop.** An email address with no context is barely a lead. The useful output is a qualified summary: what they want, timeline, budget signal, and whether they're a fit.

## What the working ones do

### 1. They're trained on your actual content

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the unglamorous core of this. Instead of relying on the model's general knowledge, you index your own material — service pages, pricing, case studies, past proposals, FAQs — and the assistant answers from that.

Practically, this means it can say "we start at $3,500 for custom website work, and for a clinic with a booking system it's usually higher — want me to check with an engineer?" instead of "our pricing varies based on your needs!"

The build cost is mostly in content preparation, not the AI. Businesses with clear, well-structured content get accurate assistants quickly. Businesses whose pricing lives in someone's head do not.

### 2. They qualify, then hand off fast

The job is not to close. It's to work out whether this person is worth a human's time, capture why, and get them booked.

A qualification flow that works looks roughly like:

- What are you trying to build or fix?
- What's driving the timeline?
- Have you got a budget range in mind?
- Would it help to talk to an engineer this week?

Four questions, conversational, no form. Then a calendar link and a summary in the CRM before the visitor closes the tab.

### 3. They know when to give up

The most valuable behaviour, and the rarest: recognising that it can't help and escalating cleanly. "I'm not certain about that one — let me get you a straight answer from the team" preserves trust. Confident nonsense destroys it, and in regulated industries it creates liability.

Set explicit refusal boundaries. Legal, medical, and financial specifics should route to a human every time.

### 4. They're measured on pipeline

Track conversations started, qualification completion rate, meetings booked, and meetings that showed up. If the only number anyone reports is "conversations handled," it's a vanity metric.

## What it costs and what it returns

A production assistant with RAG over your content, qualification logic, and CRM/calendar integration is typically a **$2,500 to $8,000** build depending on integration depth, plus model usage that usually lands under $50/month at normal small-business traffic.

The return is easier to model than most marketing spend. If your site gets 3,000 visitors a month, 2% engage the assistant, and a quarter of those complete qualification, that's 15 qualified conversations. At a 20% booking rate and a $5,000 average client value, one month covers the build.

Those numbers are illustrative, not a promise — traffic quality varies enormously. But the shape holds: this pays back through volume of qualified conversations, not through replacing staff.

## Where it genuinely doesn't fit

Be sceptical if:

- **Your traffic is very low.** Under a few hundred visitors a month, fix traffic first. An assistant with nobody to talk to returns nothing. [Technical SEO and content](/services/seo-growth-retainers) is the better first spend.
- **Your sales process is genuinely bespoke.** If every deal is a six-month consultative negotiation, a qualifier adds little.
- **Your content doesn't exist yet.** Garbage in, confident garbage out.

## The build order that works

1. Get your service, pricing, and FAQ content accurate and structured. This is most of the work.
2. Index it and test retrieval against real questions from your inbox.
3. Add qualification logic and a booking handoff.
4. Connect the CRM so context survives the conversation.
5. Instrument it, then tune the opening trigger and timing over the first month.

Skipping step one and going straight to step three is why most chatbot projects disappoint.
