"Four to six weeks" is the industry's default answer, and it's true roughly as often as it isn't. The number depends less on the build than on decisions that happen around it.

Here are honest ranges, where the time actually goes, and the five things that cause nearly every delay.

## Realistic ranges

| Project | Elapsed time | Actual working time |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page or funnel | 1–3 weeks | 3–6 days |
| Small business site (5–10 pages) | 4–8 weeks | 2–3 weeks |
| Content-heavy site (20+ pages) | 8–14 weeks | 4–6 weeks |
| Ecommerce build | 8–16 weeks | 4–8 weeks |
| Web application | 3–9 months | varies enormously |

Notice the gap between the two columns. On a typical small business site, roughly half the calendar time is spent waiting — for content, for feedback, for a decision about who signs off.

That gap is where projects are won or lost, and it's mostly under the client's control.

## Where the time goes

### Discovery and scope: 3–10 days

Working out what's being built and why. Skipping this feels fast and reliably costs more later, because you discover in week five that the booking system was assumed by one side and not the other.

### Design: 1–3 weeks

Usually two rounds. The first round establishes direction, the second refines it. A third round almost always means the brief was unclear, not that the designer failed.

### Build: 1–6 weeks

The most predictable stage, which is why estimates focus on it — and why estimates are usually wrong, since it's the stage least likely to slip.

### Content population: 3 days–forever

The single largest variable. See below.

### Testing and launch: 3–7 days

Cross-browser, mobile, forms, integrations, redirects, analytics. Compressing this is how sites launch with a broken contact form nobody notices for two weeks.

## The five things that actually cause delays

### 1. Content isn't ready

This causes more delay than everything else combined. Design can proceed on placeholder text, but the site cannot launch on it, and a build that stalls waiting for copy costs more than one that runs continuously — the team disengages and has to re-orient when it resumes.

**What to do:** write your content before the build starts, or explicitly hire copywriting as part of scope. "We'll send it over next week" is the most expensive sentence in web projects.

### 2. Too many people approve

Two decision-makers is workable. Five is a committee, and committees generate contradictory feedback that costs a revision round to reconcile.

**What to do:** name one person who can say yes. Others advise; one decides.

### 3. Scope grows quietly

"Can we also add a blog?" mid-build is not a small request — it's a template, an index, a CMS structure, and a content model.

**What to do:** keep a parked list. Additions go to phase two unless you consciously decide to extend the timeline and budget.

### 4. Third-party access appears late

Domain registrar logins, DNS, hosting, analytics, the CRM, the payment processor. Chasing credentials during launch week is routine and entirely avoidable.

**What to do:** collect every credential during discovery, not at launch.

### 5. Integrations turn out to be harder than described

"It connects to our system" can mean a documented REST API or a twenty-year-old database nobody has touched since the vendor went out of business. The difference is weeks.

**What to do:** have the developers verify integrations technically during scoping, not assume them.

## How to read a proposed timeline

Ask these:

- **What are you waiting on from me, and when?** A schedule without client dependencies listed is not a real schedule.
- **What happens if content is two weeks late?** The honest answer is "the project moves by more than two weeks," because slots get reallocated.
- **How many revision rounds are in the estimate?** Then assume you'll use them all.
- **Is launch week included?** Testing and deployment are days of real work, not an afternoon.

## Going faster, legitimately

- **Have content ready before kickoff.** The single biggest lever, worth roughly 30% of elapsed time in our experience.
- **Reduce unique layouts.** Three well-made templates beat ten bespoke pages, and it's faster and more consistent.
- **Launch smaller.** A strong eight-page site live in five weeks beats a twenty-page site live in five months. The remaining pages can ship continuously afterwards.
- **Decide fast on non-critical things.** Button shade is not worth three days.

The last one matters more than it sounds. Most delay is decision latency, not production time.
