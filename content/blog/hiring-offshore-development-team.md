We're an offshore team for most of our clients — we're in Pakistan, they're in the US, UK, and Canada. So this is written from the inside, including the parts that make us look bad.

Offshore development has a genuinely mixed reputation, and it's earned. But the failures follow a pattern, and almost none of them are about technical skill.

## Why it actually fails

### It's rarely capability

The engineering talent is real and widely distributed. Teams in Pakistan, India, Poland, Ukraine, Vietnam, and Brazil ship serious software for serious companies. If someone tells you offshore developers can't build well, they're selling you something.

### It's usually scope and communication

The four real failure modes:

**1. Ambiguity gets resolved silently.** When a requirement is unclear, a good local team asks. A remote team eight hours ahead, worried about seeming difficult, guesses — and you find out a week later. This is a cultural and structural problem, not a language one.

**2. The demo cadence is too slow.** Monthly check-ins mean a month of drift before anyone notices. Weekly is the minimum. Every two weeks is already risky.

**3. Nobody owns the outcome.** Staff-augmentation arrangements where developers implement tickets and nobody is accountable for whether the thing works produce exactly that: implemented tickets, broken product.

**4. Handover was never planned.** The project ends, the team moves on, and you have a codebase nobody can safely change. This is the most expensive failure and the easiest to prevent contractually.

## What to check before hiring

### Ask to see code, not just screenshots

A portfolio of pretty sites tells you a designer was involved. Ask for a repository you can have your own technical person look at — or if you have none, ask for their approach to tests, documentation, and code review, and see whether the answer is specific.

### Insist on a written scope with explicit exclusions

The single best predictor of a project going well. A scope that says what's *not* included has been thought about. One that only lists inclusions is a change-order machine.

### Establish the communication contract upfront

Agree, in writing:

- A weekly demo of working software, not a status document
- Overlap hours — at least three per day where both sides are awake
- One named person on each side who can make decisions
- Where questions go, and an expectation that ambiguity gets raised, not guessed

That last point deserves saying out loud at kickoff: *"If something is unclear, ask. Asking is not a failure."* It changes behaviour.

### Settle code ownership and access on day one

You should have:

- A repository **you own**, with the team as collaborators, not the other way round
- Your own hosting, domain, and infrastructure accounts
- Deployment that doesn't require any single person

If any of these live only with the vendor, you don't have a supplier — you have a dependency.

## Red flags

- **A quote without questions.** Anyone who prices a real project from a one-paragraph brief hasn't understood it.
- **A price far below everyone else.** It's not efficiency. Something is being skipped, and it's usually testing, or it's a template being sold as custom.
- **No named people.** "Our team of experts" with no individuals attached often means work gets subcontracted onward.
- **Resistance to a paid trial.** A small first engagement is the cheapest risk reduction available. A team confident in its work will welcome it.
- **Reluctance about code access.** Non-negotiable.

## Structure the engagement to fail safely

**Start small.** A defined first project — one landing page, one integration, one module — tells you more in three weeks than any number of reference calls. Pay for it properly and evaluate honestly.

**Pay against milestones, not time.** Milestones align incentives around finished work. Hourly billing on a fixed scope aligns them around hours.

**Keep a maintenance window in the contract.** 30 days post-launch where bugs are fixed at no cost. Any team that won't offer this doesn't expect the code to hold up.

**Write down what happens at the end.** Documentation, credentials transfer, and a handover call — as deliverables, not favours.

## What the time zone actually costs

Honest version: an 8–10 hour offset means asynchronous work. Handled well, that's an advantage — you review work in your morning that was done overnight, and the loop is genuinely fast.

Handled badly, every clarification costs 24 hours, and a three-question chain costs three days.

The determining factor is whether the remote team writes things down. Teams that document decisions, leave detailed pull requests, and record demos work fine across time zones. Teams that rely on quick verbal syncs don't.

Ask how they handle async communication. The answer predicts more than their portfolio.

## What we do about it

Weekly demos of working software, a named engineer you talk to directly, code in a repository you own from day one, and milestone-based payment. Not because it's generous — because these are the specific things that stop offshore projects going wrong, and we'd rather remove the risk than discount for it.
